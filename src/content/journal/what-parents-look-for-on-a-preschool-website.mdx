This is for directors, office managers and boards of small preschools and church-run early learning programs who are wondering what their website should actually say. It is not about big childcare chains with a marketing team, and it is not about enrollment software. It is about the handful of pages a worried, busy parent opens on a phone at 10pm.

The good news is that you do not have to guess what those parents want. The U.S. government publishes the questions it tells them to ask.

## What questions are parents told to ask?

Space, hours, cost, licensing, help with payment, and when they can visit.

[ChildCare.gov](https://childcare.gov/consumer-education/find-and-choose-quality-child-care/simple-steps-for-finding-and-choosing-child-care), the federal site for families, gives parents six steps for choosing care. One of them is to contact each program and ask whether it is licensed, whether it has space, what its hours and cost are, whether it accepts state child care assistance, and when they can visit. Another is to check each program's licensing status, inspection reports and quality ratings.

Read that list again as a website brief. Every one of those questions is something your site can answer before a parent ever picks up the phone. Each one you leave out is a reason to call the next preschool on the list instead.

## What should a preschool website include?

Here is the checklist I would work through with any preschool, roughly in the order parents look.

| What to show                           | Where it goes            | Why parents look for it                               |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------- |
| Book a tour or visit                   | Top of every page        | ChildCare.gov tells parents to visit several programs |
| Openings, ages and waitlist            | Home page and Admissions | "Do you have space?" is the first question            |
| Hours, calendar, closures              | Its own page             | Hours decide whether care works at all                |
| Tuition, fees, assistance accepted     | Admissions               | Cost and state assistance are on the official list    |
| License number and inspection link     | Footer and About         | Parents are told to check licensing and reports       |
| Ratios, safety, health policies        | Safety page              | Core items on Child Care Aware's checklist            |
| Teachers and director, with training   | Staff page               | Staff qualifications are a checklist category         |
| Real photos of rooms and outdoor space | Throughout               | Parents are told to look at the environment           |

Sources: [ChildCare.gov simple steps](https://childcare.gov/consumer-education/find-and-choose-quality-child-care/simple-steps-for-finding-and-choosing-child-care), [ChildCare.gov on monitoring and inspections](https://childcare.gov/consumer-education/regulated-child-care/monitoring-and-inspections), [Child Care Aware preschool checklist](https://www.childcareaware.org/families/choosing-quality-child-care/selecting-child-care-program/preschool-program-checklist/).

### Tour booking

Make it one tap. A "Schedule a visit" button in the header, on every page, that opens a short form or a booking calendar. Ask for a name, a phone or email, the child's age and preferred days. Nothing else. You can learn the rest on the visit.

### Tuition, hours and openings

This is the section preschools most often leave vague, and it is the one parents most want. You do not have to publish every fee. A clear "full day from" and "half day from" figure, the days and times you are open, and an honest line about openings ("Openings in the 3s class for fall, waitlist for 2s") answers the question that decides whether a parent calls at all. If you accept your state's child care assistance, say so plainly. ChildCare.gov lists that as one of the questions parents should ask.

### Licensing and inspection reports

[Federal law requires states to post child care monitoring and inspection results online](https://childcare.gov/consumer-education/regulated-child-care/monitoring-and-inspections), and ChildCare.gov tells parents to read them. So parents who are doing their homework will look you up anyway. Put your license number on the site and link straight to your record on your state's search tool. It shows you have nothing to hide, and it saves the parent a search.

### Safety and health

The [Child Care Aware preschool checklist](https://www.childcareaware.org/families/choosing-quality-child-care/selecting-child-care-program/preschool-program-checklist/) groups its questions into areas such as health and nutrition, supervision and safety, staff qualifications, and written policies. A short Safety page can answer the main ones in plain words: your adult-to-child ratios and group sizes, how check-in and pick-up work, how you handle allergies and medication, what your emergency plan is, and how you communicate with families during the day. Link the full parent handbook as a page, not just a PDF, so it reads well on a phone.

### Staff

Parents are choosing people, not a building. A photo and two or three sentences for each lead teacher and the director, with their training and how long they have been with you, does more than any slogan. Ask staff first and respect anyone who would rather not have their photo online.

### Photos

Use real photos of your rooms, your playground and your daily routine, with the permission your policies require. Stock photos of smiling children are easy to spot and tell a parent nothing. Pictures of children from behind, hands at work, or empty rooms set up for the morning work well when you cannot show faces.

## Why does phone-first matter so much?

Because that is where parents are. [Pew Research Center](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/) reports that 91% of U.S. adults own a smartphone, and 97% of adults aged 18 to 29 and 30 to 49, the ages of most preschool parents. Pew also found that 16% of adults have a smartphone but no home broadband, so for some families the phone is the whole internet.

| Phone check       | Pass looks like                              |
| ----------------- | -------------------------------------------- |
| Tour button       | Visible without scrolling, on every page     |
| Phone number      | Tap to call, in the header or footer         |
| Address           | Tap to open in maps                          |
| Tuition and hours | Readable as text, not a scanned flyer        |
| Forms             | Short, with big fields and no sign-up needed |

Sources: [Pew Research Center mobile fact sheet](https://www.pewresearch.org/internet/fact-sheet/mobile/), [ChildCare.gov simple steps](https://childcare.gov/consumer-education/find-and-choose-quality-child-care/simple-steps-for-finding-and-choosing-child-care).

Test it yourself. Pick up your phone, pretend you are a parent who has never heard of you, and try to find your hours, your price and how to book a visit. Time it. Then ask a friend to do the same.

## Should a preschool publish its tuition?

I think yes, at least as a starting figure or a range. Cost is one of the questions ChildCare.gov tells parents to ask, and a parent who cannot find it will often move on rather than call.

## Does a preschool need online enrollment?

Not on day one. A clear tour form and a downloadable or online application are enough for most small programs. Online enrollment and payment is worth it once the office is spending real time on paper forms.

## How often should the site be updated?

Openings, the calendar and closures should be current every term. A site that still shows last year's open house tells a parent the place is not paying attention.
