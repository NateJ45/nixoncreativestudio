This is for a church choosing between building on Squarespace, building on WordPress, or paying for a custom site. I build on WordPress and on a custom stack (Astro with Sanity), so I have a stake in this. I'll try to be straight about where Squarespace wins.

If you only need service times, a map and a giving link, skip to the section on when Squarespace is enough. You probably don't need to read the rest.

## What does each option cost?

Squarespace is the cheapest to run, WordPress varies with who hosts it, and custom costs the most up front.

| Option                | Typical cost                                | What it buys                                         |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------- |
| Squarespace           | $19 to $29 a month, billed yearly           | Templates, hosting, support                          |
| WordPress.com         | $4 to $25 a month, billed yearly            | Hosted WordPress; plugins on every paid plan         |
| Self-hosted WordPress | Varies by host and builder                  | Full control, and full responsibility                |
| Custom from me        | From $3,500, then $200 a year from year two | A site built around your church, hosting and updates |

Sources: [Squarespace pricing](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) and [WordPress.com pricing](https://wordpress.com/pricing/), read November 2026; my prices are on my [services page](/services/).

For a fuller five-year breakdown, including church platforms like Nucleus and Ekklesia 360, see my post on [how much a church website costs](/journal/how-much-does-a-church-website-cost/).

## Who edits the site day to day?

On Squarespace, anyone who can use a word processor. On WordPress and custom, it depends how the site was set up.

Squarespace's editor is visual: you click on the page and type. That is its biggest strength for churches, because the person editing changes every few years.

WordPress has a block editor that is friendly enough, but every theme and plugin adds its own screens. A volunteer can get lost when the events plugin works differently from the sermon plugin.

On my custom builds, the editing screens are made for your content. A sermon has a title, a speaker, a date and an audio link, and that is all the volunteer sees. The trade is that I design those screens, so changes to them go through me.

## Can you move the site later?

Squarespace only partly exports, WordPress moves fully, and a custom build from me goes with you.

Squarespace can [export some content as a WordPress file](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/206566687), but not everything. Its help page says calendar, store, index and several other page types don't export, nor do style settings or custom CSS. Only one blog exports. Wix is stricter: its help center says a Wix site [has to stay on Wix's servers](https://support.wix.com/en/article/exporting-or-embedding-your-wix-site-elsewhere).

WordPress is open source, so you can take the whole site to any host. Moving is a job, but it is a well-trodden one.

With me, if you ever move on, the code, content and documentation go with you.

## How do Planning Center and Church Center fit in?

All three can link to or embed Church Center, so this is less of a deciding factor than people expect.

Planning Center lets you put Church Center pages on an outside website. Its help center covers [linking or embedding Church Center pages](https://help.planningcenter.com/en/embed-or-link-your-church-center-pages.html) and adding the [donation form to your website](https://help.planningcenter.com/en/link-to-your-donation-form-on-your-website.html), either as a plain link or as a popup that keeps visitors on your site. The popup needs a script in your site's header and an https site, and on phones it opens in a new window. Planning Center also published a guide to [embedding your event calendar](https://www.planningcenter.com/blog/embed-your-event-calendar-anywhere).

Squarespace and WordPress both let you add that kind of code. On Squarespace, check which plan allows header code before you count on the popup. A custom build can pull Church Center in wherever it reads best.

If your church uses Tithe.ly instead, it offers a similar [embeddable giving form](https://help.tithe.ly/hc/en-us/articles/10799076022295-Embed-a-Tithely-Giving-Form-Onto-Your-Website-Using-an-iframe).

## Which one is most accessible?

Any of the three can be accessible or inaccessible. The template is a start, and the content decides the rest.

The standard to aim for is [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) at level AA. In practice that means real headings, alt text on photos, enough color contrast, captions on sermon videos, and a site you can use with only a keyboard.

On Squarespace and WordPress, a template sets some of that for you, and your volunteers set the rest every time they add a photo or a PDF. On a custom build, I can make the editing screens ask for alt text and stop a heading being skipped, which helps the next volunteer get it right.

Scanned PDF bulletins are the most common accessibility problem I see on church sites, on every platform. Putting the bulletin text on a web page fixes more than any theme change.

## Which one is most secure?

Squarespace handles security for you; self-hosted WordPress depends on updates; a custom static site has the least to attack.

Squarespace is a closed, hosted system, so patching is their job, and your part is a strong password and two-factor login.

WordPress runs a huge share of the web, which makes it a constant target. Most trouble comes from out-of-date plugins and themes. The official [hardening guide](https://developer.wordpress.org/advanced-administration/security/hardening/) is a good read for whoever looks after it. If nobody is assigned to updates, that is the risk.

My Astro builds are served as finished pages from Cloudflare, with the editing happening in Sanity, a separate service. There is no login page or plugin on the public site to break into. My WordPress builds get security updates under Hosting.

## When is Squarespace enough?

When the site is small, one volunteer runs it, and you don't need deep links into church software.

That describes a lot of churches. If that's you, Squarespace Core at $29 a month billed yearly is a sound choice, and I'd tell you so on a call.

## What I'd do

For a small church with one person editing, I'd pick Squarespace and spend the money elsewhere.

For a church that already lives in WordPress and has someone who keeps it updated, I'd stay on WordPress and tidy it up rather than rebuild.

For a church with a staff or volunteer team, Planning Center at the centre, and a site that has outgrown its template, I'd go custom. That's the work I do. You can see more on my [churches page](/churches/), or [ask me for a quote](/contact/).

## Is WordPress.com the same as WordPress?

No. WordPress.com is a hosted service run by one company with monthly plans. Self-hosted WordPress is the free software you install on any host you choose.

## Can I switch from Squarespace to custom later?

Yes. Expect to copy some content by hand, since Squarespace's export leaves out several page types and all styling.

## Do I need a church platform instead?

Maybe. Platforms like Nucleus or Ekklesia 360 sit between Squarespace and custom on price and come with church features built in. My cost post compares them.
