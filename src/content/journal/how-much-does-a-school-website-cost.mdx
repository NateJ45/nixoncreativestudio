This is for the head of a small private school, the director of a Christian school or preschool, or the parent who ended up running the co-op's website because nobody else would. You want real numbers before you sit through a sales demo. I've put the public prices side by side, added up five years of each, and been plain about where the vendors keep their prices to themselves.

If your school already has a site that families can find, that works on a phone, and that someone keeps current, you may not need a new one. Fixing the tuition page and the tour button is often the cheaper job.

## What are the three ways to get a school website?

You build it yourself, you rent a school platform, or you pay someone to build one for you.

A **DIY builder** (Squarespace, Wix) gives you templates and a monthly bill. You or a volunteer do the design, the writing and the upkeep.

A **school platform** (Finalsite, Edlio, Thrillshare by Apptegy, SchoolMessenger Presence, Blackbaud, Schoolpointe) is software made for schools, usually with calendars, staff pages, alerts and sometimes an app. Most are sold to public school districts by a sales team.

A **custom build** means a designer or developer makes the site around your school, and then you pay for hosting and, if you want it, ongoing help.

## What do DIY builders cost right now?

About $18 to $29 a month on a yearly plan, before your own time.

As of November 2026, the [Squarespace pricing page](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) lists Basic at $19 a month and Core at $29 a month when billed yearly, or $25 and $39 month to month. The [Wix plans page](https://www.wix.com/plans) shows Light at $17.77 and Core at $29.77 a month on yearly plans.

Tuition payments, enrollment forms and card fees usually come from another tool on top of these. Most small schools link out to whatever system already handles their billing.

Read those district figures with care. A public district buys for many schools at once, and Paradise Valley's contract covers a whole district's sites. A preschool with three classrooms would likely be quoted far less, but I can't show you a sourced number for that, so I won't guess one. If you take a demo, ask for the setup fee, the yearly fee, the length of the contract and the price at renewal, all in writing.

What the money buys is real: alerts, a staff directory, calendars that feed an app, and a support team that knows what a snow day is. That is worth paying for when a school has a communications office to run it. Most small private schools don't.

## When is DIY the right call?

When you have a willing staff member or parent with some taste and a few free evenings, DIY is the smart choice.

The preschool I volunteer for runs on Squarespace, and I kept it there on purpose. Parents already knew the editor, and the next volunteer has to be able to pick it up. I rebuilt it with Squarespace's own blocks so anyone can edit it, and next year's classes filled to 41 of 43 places. Plenty went into that besides the website, but it shows a builder site can do the job for a small school. The trade is speed: Squarespace loads a lot of its own script, and that site scores in the 40s for speed on a phone.

If your needs are a welcome page, tuition, hours, a calendar, staff photos and a way to book a tour, Squarespace or Wix will do it for under $2,000 over five years. Spend the difference on your classrooms.

DIY starts to cost more than it saves when the volunteer's last child graduates and nobody knows the login, when the tuition page is two years old because editing is fiddly, or when you need the site to connect properly with your enrollment or billing system.

## When is a school platform the better fit?

When you need alerts, an app and a calendar in one system, and you have someone on staff to run it.

A platform makes sense for a larger private school with a communications person, several campuses, or families who expect a school app. Before you sign, get the contract length and the renewal price in writing, and ask what happens to your pages and photos if you leave.

## When does a custom build make sense?

When the site has to fit how your school actually enrolls families, and you want to be able to take it with you.

That usually means a team edits it, you want tuition and the next step to be impossible to miss, accessibility matters to you, or the school has outgrown a template. For one school I built a site laid out like a printed term catalog, with its rates and scholarship note on the page instead of "contact for pricing" and a three-question finder that suggests where to start. You can see more of my school work on my [schools page](/schools/).

## What do families look for?

The same handful of answers, on a phone, fast: whether you have space, what it costs, your hours, whether you're licensed and safe, who the teachers are, and how to visit.

I wrote a full checklist in [what parents look for on a preschool website](/journal/what-parents-look-for-on-a-preschool-website/), and most of it holds for a K-12 private school or a co-op too. None of it depends on the platform. A $19 Squarespace site with honest tuition and a one-tap tour button will do better than an expensive one that hides both.

## What I'd do

If I were a preschool or co-op with a capable parent volunteer, I'd start on Squarespace Core and not look back until it stopped working.

If I were a private school of a few hundred students with an office staff, I'd ask a school platform for a written quote and compare it with a Launch or Signature quote, side by side over five years. I'd ask each one the same question: if the school leaves in year three, what does it take along? No. Enrollment and tuition usually run through a separate tool with its own fees. The website links to it or embeds its form.
