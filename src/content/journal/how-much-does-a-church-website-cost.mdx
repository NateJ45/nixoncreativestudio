This is for the pastor, church administrator or volunteer who has been told to "look into a new website" and wants real numbers before any sales calls. I've put the public prices side by side, added up five years of each, and been honest about where my own work fits and where it doesn't.

If your church already has a site that people can find, that loads on a phone, and that someone keeps current, you may not need a new one at all. A tidy-up of the content you have is often the cheaper fix.

## What are the three ways to get a church website?

You build it yourself, you rent a church platform, or you pay someone to build one for you.

A **DIY builder** (Squarespace, Wix, WordPress.com) gives you templates and a monthly bill. You do the design, the writing and the upkeep.

A **church platform** (Nucleus, Clover Sites, Ekklesia 360, Tithe.ly Sites) is a builder made for churches, with sermon pages, events and giving built in or bundled. Some include setup help.

A **custom build** means a designer or developer makes the site around your church, and then you pay for hosting and, if you want it, ongoing help.

## What do DIY builders cost right now?

Between about $4 and $30 a month on a yearly plan, before a domain and your own time.

As of November 2026, the [Squarespace pricing page](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) lists Basic at $19 a month and Core at $29 a month when billed yearly ($25 and $39 month to month). The [Wix plans page](https://www.wix.com/plans) shows Light at $17.77 and Core at $29.77 a month on yearly plans. [WordPress.com](https://wordpress.com/pricing/) starts at $4 a month for Personal, with plugins allowed on every paid plan, and its Business plan is $25 a month billed yearly.

Card fees for online giving come on top of all of these, whichever giving tool you use.

## When is DIY the right call?

When you have a willing volunteer with some taste and a few free evenings, DIY is the smart choice.

If your church is small, your needs are a welcome page, service times, a map, a few staff photos and a giving link, and someone enjoys this kind of work, Squarespace or Wix will do the job for under $2,000 over five years. Spend the difference on ministry. I mean that.

DIY starts to cost more than it saves when the volunteer moves away and nobody knows the login, when the site has drifted out of date because editing it is fiddly, or when you need it to work properly with Planning Center or another church system.

## When is a church platform the better fit?

When you want church features out of the box and a support line, and you're happy to rent rather than own.

A platform makes sense if your staff wants sermons, events and giving in one place with someone to call, and doesn't want to manage a designer. Check what happens to your content if you leave, and whether the price changes after the first year. Ask for that in writing during the demo.

## When does a custom build make sense?

When the site has to fit how your church actually works, and you want to be able to take it with you if you ever leave.

That usually means a team edits it, you rely on Planning Center or another system for events and giving, accessibility matters to you, or the church has outgrown a template. You can see the kind of church work I do on my [churches page](/churches/).

## What I'd do

If I were a small church with a capable volunteer, I'd start on Squarespace Core and not look back until it stopped working.

If I were a church of a few hundred with a staff, I'd compare a Nucleus or Ekklesia 360 demo against a Launch or Signature quote, side by side over five years, and ask each one the same question: if the church leaves in year three, what does it take along? Not in my five-year table. Domains usually cost a small yearly fee from whichever registrar you use. On my Hosting plan, I handle the renewal for you.

## Do these prices include online giving?

No. Giving runs through a tool like Planning Center Giving or Tithe.ly, and each charges its own card fees. A website links to or embeds that giving form.
