This is for the owner of a small service business: an interior designer, an embroidery studio, a contractor, a photographer, a salon or a studio of any kind. You're deciding whether to build the site yourself on Squarespace or Wix, or pay someone to build it. I build custom sites, so I have a stake in the answer. I'll try to be fair about where Squarespace wins, because for plenty of businesses it does.

If all you need is a page that says what you do, where you work and how to reach you, skip to the section on when Squarespace is the right call. That may be all you need to read.

## What does DIY do well?

It gets a good-looking site live in a weekend, for less than a nice dinner each month.

The templates are polished. The editor is visual, so you click on a heading and type. Hosting, security patches and the certificate that puts the padlock in the address bar are all handled for you. If something breaks, there is a support team whose job is to fix it.

For a business that is just starting, or one that gets most of its work from word of mouth, that is a lot of value. A tidy Squarespace site with real photos of your work beats a costly custom site that never gets finished.

I've built on Squarespace myself. The first site I made for [MAS Monograms](/work/mas-monograms/), a home embroidery studio, lived there, and it did its job.

## Where does Squarespace get in the way?

Usually in four places: quotes and bookings, speed, moving later, and how your content is shaped.

None of these is a problem on day one. They tend to show up a year or two in, once the business has grown and the site hasn't.

## Can it handle quotes and bookings?

Simple ones, yes. Work that is priced by the job is where the template starts to strain.

If customers book a fixed slot, like a one-hour consultation, a scheduling tool does the job. On Squarespace that is Acuity, which is a separate subscription from $16 a month on a yearly plan. Wix includes bookings from its Core plan up.

Custom work is harder. An embroidery order changes price with the item, the font, the thread colors, the placement and the stitch count. A template contact form asks for a name and a message, and the real details come out over a few rounds of email.

When I rebuilt the MAS Monograms site, the quote form became the center of it. It asks for the item, placement, size, thread colors, font and reference photos up front, so a request arrives ready to price. The home page also has a small studio where a customer types their initials, picks a style, fabric and thread, watches the letters stitch, and sends that exact design into the quote form. That is not something you can drag in from a template.

For [Reid Design](/work/reid-design/), an interior designer near Indianapolis, the contact form has an optional room and style picker drawn as paint chips, so the first conversation starts with the brief already in hand.

## Is a Squarespace site slow?

It can be fast, but its own help center tells you to keep each page small, which is harder than it sounds on a photo-heavy portfolio.

Squarespace's guide to [reducing page size](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/360022529371-Reducing-your-page-size-for-faster-loading) suggests keeping each page to 5 MB or less, and calls 1 MB the safest size for people on a phone signal. It also suggests fewer fonts, fewer embedded videos, and limits on gallery images and blocks per page. All good advice. But a service business usually sells with photos, and every large photo works against that limit.

On a custom build, I can resize and compress every image as part of the build itself, so the person adding photos never has to think about it. Speed matters most on phones, and most local customers will find you on one.

## Can you move a Squarespace site later?

Only partly, so in practice leaving usually means a rebuild.

Squarespace's [export help page](https://support.squarespace.com/hc/en-us/articles/206566687) says you get a WordPress-format file with your layout pages, one blog, and text and image blocks. It lists what doesn't come along: store, portfolio, index and several other page types, video and product blocks, drafts, style settings and custom CSS. Wix is stricter. Its help center says a Wix site [has to stay on Wix's servers](https://support.wix.com/en/article/exporting-or-embedding-your-wix-site-elsewhere) and can't be moved to another host.

That's not a reason to avoid either one. It's a reason to know the trade going in. Your words and photos are yours either way, but the design, the page setup and the store usually stay behind.

With a custom build from me, if you ever move on, the code, content and documentation go with you. Another developer can pick it up without starting over.

## What happened when a client left Squarespace?

The monthly platform bill went away, and the site could finally do the one job her business depends on.

Mary Ann's Squarespace plan for MAS Monograms was $22 a month. The new site runs on free hosting, so that $264 a year is gone. The bigger change was the one above: a quote form and a monogram studio built around how custom embroidery is actually priced. She can change every word and photo on the site herself.

Reid Design shows a different side. The site posts every price, from the in-home consultation to full room design, so a visitor knows what working with Staci costs before they reach out. In the six weeks after the redesign, the site got twice as many clicks from Google as in the six weeks before, and its average place in search results moved from 26 to 11. Six weeks is a short stretch, so I treat that as a good start rather than a promise.

## When is Squarespace the right call?

When the site is small, you're the one editing it, and the work comes in through a phone call or a simple booking.

That describes a lot of good businesses. If you need a home page, a services list, a gallery of your work and a contact form, and you enjoy putting it together, Squarespace Core at $29 a month billed yearly is a sound choice. I'd tell you so on a call.

It's also the right call when money is tight. A new business is better off spending $1,740 over five years on Squarespace and the rest on equipment or marketing than stretching for a custom site too early. You can move up later.

DIY starts to cost more than it saves when quotes keep turning into long email threads, when the site has drifted out of date because editing it is fiddly, or when a slow photo-heavy page is losing you people on phones.

## What I'd do

If I were starting a service business with a few clients and a tight budget, I'd build on Squarespace Core and not think about it again for a while.

If I had a steady flow of work, custom quotes that need real detail, and a site that no longer looks like the business, I'd get a quote for a Launch build and compare it against five more years of the plan I'm on, plus the hours I spend fixing it.
