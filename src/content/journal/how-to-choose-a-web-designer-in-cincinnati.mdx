This is for a church office, a school, a nonprofit or a small business in Cincinnati that needs a new website and is not sure who to hire. I am one of the people you might hire, so I have a stake in this. I'll try to be fair to the other options, because several of them are the right answer for a lot of organizations.

If you only read one section, read the ten questions. They work on anyone, me included.

## What are my options?

Roughly four: a solo designer, a local agency, a national agency, or a platform you build on yourself.

| Option                      | Good for                                                | Watch for                                          |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------- |
| Solo designer or freelancer | Smaller sites, one point of contact, lower overheads    | What happens when they are ill, busy or gone       |
| Local agency                | Bigger scope, a team with several skills, meetings      | Who actually builds it, and the size of the bill   |
| National agency             | Large or complex projects, marketing alongside the site | Small clients can become a low priority            |
| Platform you run yourself   | Tight budgets, simple sites, one confident volunteer    | Your time, template limits, and what exports later |

<Aside>

Sources: this table is my own summary of the trade-offs, not survey data. Squarespace's plans run [$19 to $29 a month billed yearly](https://www.squarespace.com/pricing) for its two cheaper tiers, read November 2026.

</Aside>

None of these is the "good" choice. A solo designer can be excellent or vanish halfway through. An agency can be worth every dollar or hand your project to a junior you never meet. The questions below are how you tell which you are dealing with.

## How do directories like Clutch and DesignRush rank designers?

Partly on reviews and experience, and partly on who pays. Both sites say so, which is to their credit.

[Clutch's methodology page](https://clutch.co/methodology) says its rank comes from an "Ability to Deliver" score, built mostly from verified reviews, plus how focused a company is on the service. It also says sponsored listings appear above organic results by default, and that sponsorship does not change the underlying score. Its help center says the same: [sponsors are listed above non-sponsors](https://help.clutch.co/en/knowledge/difference-between-sponsor-and-non-sponsor) on directory pages, and you can sort by Clutch Rank to see the list without the paid order.

[DesignRush's category pages](https://www.designrush.com/agency/digital-agencies/us) say organic rankings come from five factors: top services, reviews, portfolio, awards and press, and team expertise. They also say sponsored listings pay for a placement above organic positions, and that sponsorship does not affect star ratings or review content.

So the first few names you see on a "best web designers in Cincinnati" page may be there because they paid to be. That does not make them bad. Many good studios sponsor listings to be found. It just means the order is an advert, not a verdict. Re-sort by rank where you can, read the reviews themselves, and look at the actual sites in each portfolio.

The same goes for any "top 10" list: ask whether the people on it paid, and how the list was made. A list that will not say is telling you something.

## Which ten questions should I ask?

These work on a freelancer, an agency or a platform salesperson. Ask them in writing and keep the answers.

1. **Who will actually build it?** The person on the sales call, a team in the office, or a subcontractor you never meet? Any of these can work. You just deserve to know.
2. **Who will I talk to week to week?** One named person, and how fast do they usually reply?
3. **Who registers the domain, and who holds the accounts?** That means the domain, the hosting, analytics and any plugins. Some designers run these for you on purpose, which is fine, as long as the answer is clear and written down. [ICANN explains](https://www.icann.org/news/blog/do-you-have-a-domain-name-here-s-what-you-need-to-know-part-2) that only the registered name holder or the administrative contact can start a transfer, so know who that is.
4. **How would I leave?** Ask what you get if you part ways: the code, the content, the documentation, and how long the handover takes. This matters more than who owns what on day one.
5. **Will it meet WCAG 2.2 AA, and how do you test that?** [WCAG 2.2](https://www.w3.org/TR/WCAG22/) is the current accessibility standard. Public schools and other public bodies have a legal deadline: the Department of Justice's Title II rule requires WCAG 2.1 AA, and an [interim rule in April 2026 moved the dates](https://campustechnology.com/articles/2026/04/27/doj-extends-deadline-for-ada-title-ii-compliance.aspx) to April 26, 2027 for larger entities and April 26, 2028 for smaller ones. A good answer names the tools and includes testing with a keyboard.
6. **How fast will it be, and how will I know?** Google's [Core Web Vitals](https://web.dev/articles/defining-core-web-vitals-thresholds) call a page good when it shows its main content within 2.5 seconds, responds to a tap within 200 milliseconds and barely shifts as it loads. Ask for those numbers on a site they have already built.
7. **What will hosting and care cost over five years?** The build is one line. Hosting, updates, support and changes are the rest. Ask for the total, year by year.
8. **Who edits it after launch, and how?** If your volunteer or office manager will make changes, ask to see the editing screens before you sign, not after.
9. **What happens if something breaks at 9pm on a Saturday?** Who notices, who fixes it, and what it costs.
10. **Can I talk to two clients from the last year or two?** Not testimonials on a page. People you can call.

## What are the red flags?

Most are about vagueness rather than price. A cheap quote is not a red flag on its own.

- **A price with no list of what it includes.** You cannot compare it with anything.
- **No straight answer on leaving.** If "what do I get if I leave?" gets a pause, ask again until you get a clear one.
- **A guarantee of first place on Google.** Nobody controls that, and [Google's own advice on hiring an SEO](https://developers.google.com/search/docs/fundamentals/do-i-need-seo) says no one can guarantee a number one ranking.
- **An accessibility "overlay" offered as the fix.** A script that sits on top of a site does not replace building it properly. Ask how they test.
- **No live examples.** Screenshots are fine, but you should be able to open a few real sites they built and use them on your phone.
- **Pressure to sign this week.** A real discount does not usually expire on Friday.
- **Nobody can tell you who writes the words.** Content is the slowest part of most projects. Somebody has to own it, and it is often you.

## Does it matter if the designer is local?

Sometimes a lot, sometimes not at all.

It helps when you want photos of your own people and buildings rather than stock images. A designer who also shoots can come to a Sunday service, a school morning or your shop floor. I do that across the Cincinnati region, from $1,200 for a half day. It also helps when a room full of people needs to agree: a building committee, a board or a staff team often decides faster sitting at one table than on a video call. My projects open with an hour together in person or on video, and in Cincinnati it can be either.

It matters less when the job is mainly technical, when you are happy on video calls, or when the best fit for your sector happens to live in another state. A good designer two time zones away beats a poor one down the road. Being local is a reason to shortlist someone, not a reason to skip the ten questions.

## What would I do in your shoes?

For a very small organization with one confident volunteer and simple needs, I would start on a platform like Squarespace and spend the money elsewhere.

For a larger project with lots of moving parts, marketing alongside the site and a budget to match, I would talk to a local or national agency, and ask question one early.

For a church, school, nonprofit or small business that wants a custom site, someone to talk to directly, and help afterwards, a solo designer is worth a look. That is the work I do. ## Should I get more than one quote?

Yes. Two or three is plenty. Send each the same short brief so the quotes are comparable.

## Is the cheapest quote a mistake?

Not always. Compare what each one includes, and the five-year cost, before you compare the totals.

## Do I need a designer who knows my sector?

It helps, mostly because they will already know the tools you use and the questions your visitors ask. Ask for an example in your sector.
