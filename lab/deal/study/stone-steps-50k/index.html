The brief

A rebuild for Cincinnati's longest-running ultramarathon: every result since 2003, records calculated from them, and a 3D map of the course measured from a real run.

The Stone Steps 50K is a trail ultramarathon through Mt. Airy Forest in Cincinnati: seven loops out of The Oval, a 27K for runners who want four, and a loyal community that has come back every October since 2003. Race director David Corfman has run it for most of that time.

I built the race's first real website in 2024, on WordPress. It gave the race a home, but two years on its weak spots were clear. The records lived in hand-typed tables that had started to contradict each other. The course was summed up by a single elevation figure with no measurement behind it written down anywhere.

So the brief for version two was about trust as much as looks. Every number on the site should come from a source you can point to, the history should be complete, and the site should keep itself current after each race without anyone typing anything in.

Decisions

Records from the results

The easy version
Carry the hand-typed records tables over to the new site and keep them up by hand.
What I did
Start from the results. Every finish since 2003 is its own entry, with the time stored as a number and the finish linked to a runner profile, and the course records, age-group records and top-ten lists are all calculated from those entries.

The course, measured instead of described

The easy version
Keep the old total, 10,726 feet of elevation change, a number nobody had written down a source for.
What I did
Draw the course map from Dave's own recorded run, on real 3D terrain, and measure the climbing along that same track: about 5,200 feet, from USGS 1-meter LiDAR, with the method stated right next to the chart.

A look taken from the race's own sign

The easy version
A near-black and blaze orange scheme that looks like a trail race. That was my first draft.
What I did
Throw it out, because it didn't look like this race, and start from the logo: a hand-painted trail sign in cream and gold. The palette is sampled from the logo file, and the buttons are sign plates.

What the weather has done

Every race morning since 2003 as a temperature range, with rain on the day and rain the night before marked. Under it, a what-to-pack note reads the same record: the usual start temperature, how much it warms up by the afternoon, and how many years the steps were wet. It replaces the live forecast widget from the first version with something only this race has, and it adds each new year on its own.

The site keeps itself current. Race details live in a Sanity Studio that Dave can edit himself, with a checklist for rolling the site over to a new year. After each race, a nightly job pulls the new results from RunSignUp, and by the next morning the results page, the records, the runner pages, and the edition count are all up to date. If he doesn't want to wait for the night, there is a "Fetch the latest results" button on the Studio's home screen, and a correction from the timer rebuilds the site by itself. Trekkers who take the optional early start are marked in the results, so they never land on an awards list by mistake. The contact form delivers straight to the race director's inbox, and the whole site moved onto Cloudflare along with its domain. Every change is checked against a perfect Lighthouse accessibility score, and the check fails if it drops.

The full build:

  • Homepage with the race date, a countdown to the gun, both distances with their fees and start times, a race-morning timetable, and registration through RunSignUp
  • A course preview on the homepage: pick the long or short loop and run your cursor along its profile
  • Interactive 3D course map with a flyover, satellite and topo views, a steepness view, and an elevation scrubber
  • A results page for every year from 2003 to 2026, plus a page for each runner showing every finish on file
  • All-time records and top-ten lists for both distances, calculated from the results
  • Historical race-day weather for every edition, with what to pack going by the record
  • Parking and directions to the start, a travel map with hotels downtown and near the airport, the common questions answered, and a contact form that goes straight to the race director
  • A Sanity Studio for the race director, with automatic results imports after each race

Behind the decisions

Hand-kept tables are how the same 1:58:36 ended up listed under both 2010 and 2011 on the old site. Calculating the records meant finding the results first. The recent years came straight from RunSignUp's public API. The early ones came from the race's archived results pages, and every year was re-checked against its source. That check caught real mistakes: an importer that misread one year's column for sex and left 2004 without its three women, and twelve old 27K "records" that matched no finish in any year. Those came off the board, and the question of where they came from went back to Dave.

The old course section was a race photo, a scan of the park's trail map, and a paragraph of loop directions. On the new map, visitors can drag, tilt, switch between satellite and topo, color the route by steepness, and fly the whole course. The elevation profile below the map doubles as a scrubber, so hovering over a climb shows exactly where it is on the course.

Course records are names stamped onto a bark board, and each year's results page is laid out like a timing sheet on a clipboard.
